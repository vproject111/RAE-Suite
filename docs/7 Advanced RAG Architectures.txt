Ithought I understood RAG. My first pipeline was simple: chunk the documents, create embeddings, retrieve the top few matches, put them into the prompt, and let the LLM answer.

And honestly, it looked great. The demo worked. The answers sounded convincing. I thought I was done.

Then I started asking it questions that actually mattered.

Suddenly, it would pull the wrong chunk. Sometimes it found the right document but missed the part I needed. Other times, the information was sitting in the database, yet the model somehow never saw it.

The weirdest part?

Nothing was broken.

The pipeline was running exactly as I had built it.

That was when I realized something most RAG tutorials don’t make obvious: getting documents into a vector database is the easy part. Getting the right information into the model’s context is the real problem.

What I had built was Naive RAG. And Naive RAG isn’t useless. It’s where you start.

But once your documents get messy, your questions get harder, or your application needs to work reliably, a single retrieve-and-answer loop starts showing its limits.

That’s where the more interesting RAG architectures come in.

In this article, I’ll break down seven of them, what problem each one solves, where it can fail, and most importantly, how to know which one your own RAG system actually needs.

What RAG actually is, in plain terms
An LLM only knows what it learned during training.

Ask it about your company’s internal documents, or anything recent, and it either admits it doesn’t know, or worse, makes something up that sounds confident.

RAG fixes this the way an open-book exam beats a closed-book one.

Before the model answers, your system looks up relevant information first and hands it to the model as reference material, instead of asking the model to answer purely from memory.

Where naive RAG breaks down
The basic RAG pipeline is actually pretty simple:

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Photo from link
Before looking at the advanced architectures, let’s quickly see what a basic RAG pipeline actually does.

When a user asks a question, the system:

Searches your external knowledge base for relevant information.
Retrieves the most relevant chunks.
Adds those chunks to the prompt alongside the user’s question.
Sends everything to the LLM, which generates the answer using that context.
In simple terms: Question → Search → Retrieve → Add context → LLM → Answer

This works surprisingly well when your documents are small and clean. Real-world data is rarely that simple.

Chunks can lose context. A piece saying “revenue grew 3%” might not say which company or quarter it belongs to.
Important information can be missed. Tables, charts, and scanned pages don’t always survive text-based retrieval.
The first search can be wrong. Basic RAG usually doesn’t stop and try a different search.
Previous conversations disappear. Without memory, every new session starts from zero.
Facts can stay disconnected. Information spread across multiple documents may need to be connected before the answer makes sense.
These aren’t five random problems. They’re the reason different RAG architectures exist.

Each architecture below tackles a different failure in this basic pipeline.

1. Contextual RAG
Press enter or click to view image in full size

Photo from AI
Before you store a chunk of text, you attach a short note explaining what that chunk is about, so it still makes sense once it’s been pulled out of its original document.

A simple way to picture it: Imagine tearing a single paragraph out of a book and handing it to a stranger with no title, no chapter number, nothing. They’d have no idea what it’s referring to.

Now imagine stapling a small note to that page first: “This is from Chapter 4, about the company’s Q2 earnings.” That’s the whole idea.

Raw chunk:        "revenue grew 3% over the previous quarter"
                   -> which company? which year? unclear

With a note attached:  "From ACME Corp's Q2 2023 filing.
                        Revenue grew 3% over the previous quarter."
                   -> now it makes sense on its own
Pros:

Chunks retrieved later make sense on their own, instead of arriving stripped of context.
Doesn’t require changing your search method, just what gets stored before you search.
Cons:

Writing a note for every single chunk costs time and money before you can even start searching.
A badly written note can point the search in the wrong direction just as easily as a good one points it in the right one.
Once your chunks carry their own context, the next problem is documents that aren’t plain text at all.

2. Multimodal RAG
Press enter or click to view image in full size

Photo from AI
Instead of converting a PDF page into text first and then searching that text, you let the system look at the page itself, as an image, so tables and charts don’t get mangled in translation.

A simple way to picture it: Think about the difference between reading someone’s written description of a chart and just looking at the chart yourself.

The description can leave out details or get the numbers slightly wrong. Looking at the real thing doesn’t have that problem.

Old way:  PDF page -> extract text -> guess at the table layout -> search the text
          (numbers and structure can get scrambled along the way)

Multimodal way:  PDF page -> search the page as an image directly
                 (the table stays a table, the chart stays a chart)
Pros:

Handles tables, charts, and scanned pages that text extraction tends to garble.
Removes a fragile step in the pipeline, since there’s no text extraction to get wrong.
Cons:

Needs more computing power than searching plain text.
Still newer, with fewer tools and less community knowledge to lean on than text search.
3. Hybrid Search RAG
Press enter or click to view image in full size

Photo from AI
You run two kinds of search at once, one that understands meaning, and one that matches exact words, then combine the results.

A simple way to picture it: Imagine two librarians helping you. One is great at understanding what you mean, even if you don’t use the exact right words.

The other doesn’t understand context at all, but she’ll find the exact document with that one specific code you typed, every time. You want both of them on the case.

Query: "error E0502 borrow checker"

Meaning-based search:   finds docs about a similar concept, even without that exact phrase
Exact-word search:      finds the literal string "E0502"
Combined:               merges both into one final list
Pros:

Catches both loose, conceptual questions and exact technical terms in one search.
Fixes a real, common blind spot: meaning-based search alone is often bad with codes, IDs, and jargon.
Cons:

You’re now running and maintaining two search systems instead of one.
Deciding how much weight to give each system’s results takes real tuning.
4. GraphRAG
Press enter or click to view image in full size

Source: https://rabiloo.com/blog/graph-rag-the-upgrade-that-traditional-rag-needed
Instead of storing isolated chunks, you map out how the people, companies, and ideas in your documents actually connect to each other, group related ones together, and write a short summary for each group ahead of time.

When a question is too big for any single chunk to answer, the system pulls from those group summaries instead, and can even decide on its own which groups are worth checking.

A simple way to picture it: Say you want to know “what are the big themes across everything we’ve published this year.” No single article answers that. But if your company were already split into departments, and each department had written a one-page summary of what they worked on, you could read just those summaries and get the answer fast. That’s the map.

The smarter version of this same idea is having an assistant who first figures out which departments are relevant to your question, instead of reading every department’s summary whether it matters or not.

Step 1, build the map (done once, ahead of time):

  Documents -> find how people/ideas connect -> group related ones
            -> write a short summary for each group

Step 2, answer a broad question:

  "What are the big themes here?"
  -> pick the relevant groups (a smart version does this pick automatically)
  -> read just their summaries -> combine into one answer
Pros:

Answers big, thematic questions that no single chunk could ever cover on its own.
Can trace a connection across many different documents at once, not just search one at a time.
The smarter, self-selecting version avoids wasting time reading summaries that don’t matter to the question asked.
Cons:

Building the map in the first place is a real project, not a quick setup step.
Every time your documents change meaningfully, parts of that map need to be rebuilt.
Letting the system pick which groups matter adds one more decision it can get wrong.
Mapping out a whole document set solves the “big picture” question. But some questions need more than one search no matter how good your map is.

5. Agentic RAG
Press enter or click to view image in full size

Source: https://bhavishyapandit9.substack.com/p/25-types-of-rag-part-1
Instead of searching once and answering with whatever came back, the system is allowed to search, look at what it got, decide if that’s enough, and search again if it isn’t, as many times as it takes.

A simple way to picture it: Think of the difference between a search engine and a research assistant. A search engine gives you one list of results and stops.

A good research assistant reads what they find, realizes it’s not quite enough, and goes back to look for more, using what they already learned to search smarter the second time.

Question comes in
     -> Search once -> "Is this enough to answer?"
     -> No: search again, using what was just learned
     -> No, still not enough: search again, from a different angle
     -> Yes: now answer
Pros:

Handles questions that genuinely need more than one lookup to answer properly.
Doesn’t give up after one bad search the way a fixed pipeline does.
Cons:

Every extra search means another round trip, which adds real cost and waiting time.
If it decides on the wrong angle to search from, it can burn several attempts down the wrong path before catching the mistake.
6. Multi-Agent RAG
Press enter or click to view image in full size

Photo from AI
Instead of one system trying to plan, search, check, and write all by itself, you split those jobs across several smaller, specialized systems that each do one thing well, coordinated by one that manages the whole process.

A simple way to picture it: One person trying to research a topic, write it up, and fact-check their own writing all at once tends to do a mediocre job at all three.

A small team where one person plans the research, another does the digging, another checks it for accuracy, and another writes the final piece, tends to do better at each individual part.

Question comes in
  -> Planner decides what needs to be looked up
  -> Searchers go look, sometimes from different sources at the same time
  -> Checker reviews what came back for accuracy
  -> Writer puts together the final answer
Pros:

Each part of the job gets full attention, instead of one system trying to juggle everything at once.
Several searches can happen at the same time instead of one after another, saving time.
Cons:

More systems working together means more cost per question.
When the final answer is wrong, tracing the mistake back through several steps takes real effort.
7. Memory RAG
Press enter or click to view image in full size

Source: https://humanloop.com/blog/rag-architectures
Instead of only searching a fixed set of documents, the system also searches its own history with you, treating past conversations as something it can look back on, not something it forgets the moment the conversation ends.

A simple way to picture it: Think about the difference between a doctor who reads your file before every visit and one who asks you to explain your entire medical history from scratch every single time. Most of what you told them last visit is still sitting in a folder somewhere.

A good system works the same way: it keeps the important stuff on hand and looks it up again when it’s relevant, rather than starting over each time.

Pros:

Lets a system remember things you mentioned days or weeks ago, without needing to keep everything loaded at once.
Genuinely useful for anything personal or ongoing, not just one-off document lookups.
Cons:

You’re now managing a memory system on top of a document search system, which is more moving parts.
Older memories sometimes get compressed or summarized to save space, and some detail can get lost in that process.

The one thing worth remembering
Naive RAG doesn’t fail loudly. It doesn’t crash or throw an error. It just hands your users an answer built on the wrong information, and everything looks fine until someone checks.

Every architecture on this list exists because someone traced a wrong answer back to one specific, fixable cause.

Don’t reach for the most advanced option on this list just because it’s the newest. Reach for the one that matches the specific way your retrieval is breaking.

What to do today
Look at your current RAG pipeline and ask one honest question: when it gives a wrong answer, is it because the search found the wrong thing, because a chunk lost its context, or because the system never looked twice? That question alone points you straight at which architecture on this list you need next.
